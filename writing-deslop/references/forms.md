# Form-specific rules

The general catalog in [tropes.md](tropes.md) was built from expository writing. This file says what to add, relax, and protect for each form. Read the section that matches the piece; the same density rule applies everywhere: one occurrence is style, a cluster is a tell.

## 1. Blog posts and articles

The general catalog applies in full. These are the extra tells of the form.

### Openers

- **The throat-clear**: "In today's fast-paced world", "In the ever-evolving landscape of", "In recent years", "Have you ever wondered". Start with the first real sentence.
- **The audience sweep**: "Whether you're a seasoned pro or just starting out". Name the actual reader or drop it.
- **The promise paragraph**: "In this post, we'll explore X, Y, and Z" followed by sections titled X, Y, and Z. Cut it; the headings already say this.
- **Definition lead**: opening with a dictionary-style definition nobody asked for.

### Body

- **Formula headings**: "X: Why Y Matters", "The Power of X", "Unlocking X", "X 101", a question as every heading. Write what the section says.
- **Listicle padding**: seven items because the title promised seven, three of them the same point. Merge or cut.
- **Borrowed authority**: "studies show", "experts agree" with no link. Link the source that is already in the piece or cut the claim. Never add one.
- **Fake anecdote shape**: "Picture this: Sarah, a busy marketing manager…". An invented everyperson in place of a real example. Cut unless the author supplied a real one.
- **SEO echo**: the title keyword repeated in every heading and first sentence.
- **Emoji bullets and section emoji**, unless the blog's established style uses them.

### Closers

- **The recap**: a "Key takeaways" or "Final thoughts" section that restates the post. Keep only if it adds a decision or next step.
- **The engagement beg**: "What do you think? Let me know in the comments!" Remove unless the author wrote it.
- **The uplift**: "The future of X is bright", "The journey is just beginning". End on the last substantive point.

### Protect

- First-person anecdotes, opinions, jokes, and asides. These are the human part; do not trim them for concision.
- The author's established register, including profanity, slang, and regional spelling.
- Deliberate informality: sentences starting with "And" or "But", one-word reactions, contractions.

## 2. Fiction and narrative

Rhetoric that is a tell in an essay is often craft in a story. Relax the general catalog first, then hunt the fiction-specific tells.

### Relax

- **Fragments, repetition, anaphora, rule of three**: legitimate when they carry rhythm or a character's state of mind. Flag only when every paragraph does it.
- **Em dashes**: standard for interrupted speech and broken thought. Do not cap by count; flag only decorative ones in narration.
- **Curly quotes**: correct for published prose. Leave them.
- **Metaphor and simile**: wanted. Flag stock ones and stacked ones, not their existence.
- **Plain-phrasing bias**: does not apply. A voice may be ornate. Judge against the piece's own voice, not against neutral prose.
- **Negative parallelism and rhetorical questions in dialogue**: people talk this way. Flag only when several characters do it, or the narrator does it constantly.

### Stock phrases

Over-represented in model-written fiction. Replace with something specific to this character and moment, or cut.

- **Breath and heart**: "a breath she didn't know she was holding", "let out a shaky breath", "heart pounding in her chest", "heart skipped a beat", "pulse quickened".
- **Eyes and faces**: "eyes glinted", "eyes sparkled with mischief", "something flickered in his eyes", "a smile tugged at the corner of her mouth", "jaw clenched", "brow furrowed".
- **Voice**: "barely above a whisper", "voice dripping with", "said, a hint of X in her voice", "voice thick with emotion".
- **Spine and skin**: "shivers down her spine", "a chill ran through him", "goosebumps".
- **Atmosphere**: "palpable tension", "the air crackled", "the air was thick with", "ethereal glow", "a symphony of", "a tapestry of", "a testament to", "the smell of ozone".
- **Hedged interiority**: "couldn't help but", "found herself", "something shifted", "a mix of X and Y", "a pang of".
- **Stock names and places**: the same handful of fantasy and sci-fi names models reach for by default. If the author did not choose the name, ask or flag it; do not rename on your own.

### Structural tells

- **Named emotions**: "She felt a wave of sadness." State what she does, notices, or says instead, using detail already in the text. If nothing is there, leave the line and flag it in review mode.
- **Summary instead of scene**: important moments reported in a sentence ("They argued for hours") while trivial ones get full description. Flag; do not invent the missing scene.
- **One voice for everyone**: every character articulate, self-aware, speaking in complete composed paragraphs. Differentiate using traits the text already establishes.
- **Therapy-speak**: characters naming their own feelings and motives with clinical clarity ("I think I've been pushing people away because I'm afraid"). Cut back to what this person would say aloud.
- **As-you-know dialogue**: characters telling each other what both already know, for the reader's benefit.
- **Adverb-loaded tags**: "she said softly", "he replied grimly" on most lines. Prefer "said" or an action beat.
- **Instant resolution**: conflict raised and soothed in the same scene; antagonists who turn reasonable; arguments that end in mutual understanding. Flag it. Fixing it is a plot change and belongs to the author.
- **The reflective closer**: a final paragraph where the character looks at the horizon and realizes what it all meant, or "for the first time in years, she felt hope". End on the last concrete image or line of action.
- **Portentous section endings**: every scene closing on a one-line omen ("Little did she know…", "Nothing would ever be the same").
- **Sensory checklist**: sight, sound, smell delivered in order in every scene opening.
- **Metronomic paragraphs**: action sentence, reaction sentence, interior thought, repeat.

### Protect

- Plot, events, order of scenes, point of view, tense, and canon facts.
- Character voice, dialect, verbal tics, and deliberately bad or purple speech.
- The author's chosen level of darkness, explicitness, and moral ambiguity. Do not soften, add a lesson, or add reassurance.
- Poetry: line breaks, meter, and repetition are structure. Edit word choice only, and only when asked.

## 3. Role play and interactive fiction

The unit of slop here is the reply, and the pattern across replies, more than the sentence. Read prior turns before judging.

### Per-reply tells

- **Fixed reply shape**: every turn is action, then dialogue, then a question; or always exactly three paragraphs. Vary the shape with what the moment needs. A one-line reply is sometimes right.
- **The trailing prompt**: ending every reply with "What do you do?", "What will you say?", or an in-character question that exists only to hand the turn back. End where the beat ends.
- **Acting for the other player**: stating what their character does, says, feels, or decides. Describe only your own character and the world's response.
- **Recap opening**: restating what the other player just wrote before responding to it.
- **Echoing**: quoting their words back ("'A dragon?' she repeated").
- **Narrating instead of playing**: summarizing what the character would do ("She would probably be suspicious") in place of doing it.
- **Out-of-character leakage**: disclaimers, safety notes, "as an AI", commentary on the story, or offers to continue, when no out-of-character channel was opened.
- **Length creep**: replies growing longer every turn regardless of what the scene needs, or far longer than the other player's.
- **Menu endings**: closing with a list of options the other player could take, unless the game is explicitly choice-based.

### Across-turn tells

- **Positivity drift**: a gruff, hostile, shy, or villainous character sliding into warmth and agreeableness. Hold the persona as defined.
- **Voice drift**: the character's speech pattern, vocabulary, or accent fading into neutral narrator prose.
- **Repeated descriptors**: the same gesture every turn (the smirk, the raised eyebrow, the tilted head, the low chuckle).
- **Name overuse**: addressing the other character by name in nearly every line.
- **Stalling**: turns that describe mood and scenery but change nothing. Each reply should move something: a fact revealed, a choice made, a consequence landed.
- **Yes-anding everything**: no resistance, cost, or surprise from the world or other characters.
- **Continuity loss**: forgetting established facts, injuries, inventory, location, or who knows what. Check against prior turns.

### Stock phrases

The fiction list above applies. Role play adds: "a mischievous glint", "a smirk playing on his lips", "voice low and husky", "closing the distance between them", "the ball is in your court", "the night is still young", "we've only just begun", "you have no idea", "shall we?".

### Protect

- The scene's established format: asterisks or italics for actions, quote style, first or third person, tense, reply length norms, any status blocks or dice notation.
- The persona or character definition. It outranks this catalog, including when it calls for a florid, repetitive, or abrasive voice.
- The other player's agency and their character's established traits.
- Rating and tone set by the participants. Do not soften or redirect the scene.
- In-world documents, songs, spells, and prophecy, which may be deliberately archaic or ornate.

## 4. Docs, READMEs, and email

Listed for completeness; the general catalog covers them.

- Register is the main risk: marketing voice in a README ("Unlock the power of…"), magazine prose in a commit message or chat reply.
- Keep bold, tables, and headings where they aid scanning. Reference material is allowed to be structured.
- Do not rewrite code, commands, quoted errors, or anything in a code block.
